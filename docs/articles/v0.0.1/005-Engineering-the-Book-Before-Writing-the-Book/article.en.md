# Engineering the Book Before Writing the Book

[Medium](https://tareknajem04.medium.com/ai-assisted-professional-engineering-with-net-ee040aeb8e1b)
[LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/ai-assisted-professional-engineering-net-tarek-najem-fxpbe)

Most authors begin by writing.

I began by building.

That decision puzzled even me at first.

Before a single chapter was complete, I was already designing directory structures, defining documentation conventions, automating repetitive tasks, experimenting with publishing pipelines, and documenting engineering decisions.

From the outside, it looked like I was delaying the real work.

In reality, I was preparing the environment in which the real work could continue for years.

Software engineers understand this instinct well.

Before implementing a large system, we invest in architecture.

We establish coding standards.

We create build pipelines.

We automate testing.

We document design decisions.

We know that a strong foundation accelerates future development.

Yet many technical writing projects ignore the same principle.

The focus is often placed on producing pages as quickly as possible.

The infrastructure around the content is treated as something that can be improved later.

I chose the opposite approach.

The manuscript would be only one component of a larger engineering system.

The publishing platform had to exist before large-scale writing began.

Automation had to remove repetitive work.

Documentation had to explain not only what decisions were made, but why they were made.

The repository itself had to communicate the project's identity.

That early investment often felt expensive.

Some days produced no new chapters.

Some weeks produced only scripts, documentation, or architectural diagrams.

At first glance, progress appeared slow.

But every improvement multiplied the value of everything written afterward.

Writing became faster.

Publishing became predictable.

Refactoring became safer.

New ideas integrated naturally instead of increasing complexity.

Looking back, I don't believe I delayed writing the book.

I believe I engineered the conditions that made writing the book sustainable.

That distinction matters.

Because engineering is rarely about reaching the finish line as quickly as possible.

It is about designing systems that continue moving long after the initial excitement disappears.

Perhaps the most valuable lesson from this project is that knowledge deserves engineering too.

We routinely engineer software before writing code.

Why shouldn't we engineer knowledge before writing books?
